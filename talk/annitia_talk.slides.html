LLM Risk Rankers and Agentic Scaffolding for Liver Disease Survival Modeling¶

A case study: the ANNITIA 2026 challenge. Predict major hepatic events and all-cause death from irregular longitudinal records of MASLD patients (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease).

Every cell that runs here uses a synthetic cohort or our own aggregate scores. No challenge record appears in this notebook, and a guard enforces that.

The argument, in one sentence¶

A reasoning LLM that reads a patient's history as clinical text is a weak survival model on its own. On the leaderboard it seemed to improve a strong tabular ensemble, because it makes different mistakes. This talk shows why that was plausible, and how a leak-free replication overturned it.

1 · Objective: the metric only sees order¶

Score = 0.7 × C-index(hepatic) + 0.3 × C-index(death).

Harrell's C-index asks one question of every comparable pair: did the patient with the earlier event get the higher risk? Scale, calibration and units are invisible to it.

2 · The data: wide, irregular and censored¶

One row per patient, up to 22 visits, each with biomarkers such as ALT, platelets, FibroScan stiffness and FibroTest. Visits are irregular, non-invasive tests are often missing, and most patients never have the event (right censoring).

3 · The tabular base: accelerated failure time boosting¶

XGBoost AFT and CatBoost SurvivalAft, rank blended per endpoint and averaged over 10 seeds. The real features are trajectory summaries (first, last, slope, volatility), clinical scores such as FIB-4 and APRI, and missingness patterns. Here is a trimmed version on the synthetic cohort.

def features(df):
    rows = []
    for _, r in df.iterrows():

A model that beats the true risk is reading the answer. Follow-up stops at the event, so last values, slopes and even whether a slope exists all encode how long follow-up lasted. The baseline-only model sits just under the truth, as an honest model should.

The pipeline masked visits after the event and dropped follow_up_years and Age_delta. But it kept Age_first and Age_last, and their difference is the follow-up time. Section 8 shows what that did to our CV. The loader now drops every window feature. Transfer: compare every model against an oracle or a baseline-only model.

Split endpoints: one prompt per question¶

Hepatic events are driven by fibrosis. Death is driven mostly by age and cardiometabolic load. One combined prompt blurs the two, so each endpoint gets its own prompt and its own score.

The model is GPT-OSS 120B, served through Ollama. Its 0-100 outputs are treated as rankings, not probabilities, which is all a C-index needs.

The scaffolding around the LLM¶

The model sees one prompt and returns one JSON object. Everything else is a harness we wrote: it decides what the model may see, keeps a long run alive on free API tiers, and records every score so a crash costs nothing. It is a fixed pipeline, not an autonomous agent: the model picks no tools and no next steps. Shown here: the harness behind the leak-free replication (talk/helm_train_scores.py).

5 · Why a weak ranker helps: it is the error correlation¶

Two simulated rankers of the same true risk. The tabular one is strong. The LLM one is weaker and coarse (20 distinct values, like real LLM scores). We vary one knob: how correlated their mistakes are.

6 · Check rank correlation before spending API calls¶

Scoring every patient costs two LLM calls each. Two cheap diagnostics, both printed by our blend script, say early whether a new ranker is worth it:

  • Spearman ρ between its ranks and the base ranks: lower means more new information.
  • Unique values: a ranker with a handful of distinct scores ties most pairs.

8 · Gates that say yes too easily¶

A false-positive gate declares a change real when it is not. Our gate was the public leaderboard, and it is deterministic: re-uploading a file gives the identical score. So the risk is not measurement noise. It is overfitting a fixed sample of 127 patients (30% of 423).

A change passes only if it clears all five criteria:

  1. Resolution: the gain beats 2 paired standard errors on the gate's own sample.
  2. Multiplicity: it beats the best of K null tries, about SE·√(2 ln K).
  3. Mechanism: it is no larger than error independence can deliver.
  4. Ceiling: the score stays below what is attainable.
  5. Replication: it shows up on independent evidence.

3 · Mechanism: how much gain can complementarity deliver?¶

Model each ranker as truth plus noise of sd s. For two rankers with error correlation r, the best blend puts value weight (s₁² − r s₁s₂) / (s₁² + s₂² − 2r s₁s₂) on the second, and its noise variance is s₁²s₂²(1 − r²) / (s₁² + s₂² − 2r s₁s₂). Each standalone C-index fixes its s. First we check the formula against simulation.

Assumptions: a ceiling C-index of 0.95 maps each score to a noise level, and the weighted metric is treated as one endpoint.

4 · Ceiling and 5 · Replication¶

Ceiling. A score above what is attainable is a leak, not a win. Compare against the true risk in simulation, a baseline-only model, or a red-flag threshold. Section 2 showed a model at 0.914 against a true risk of 0.825. ablation_finetune_efficientnet.py flags CV above 0.947.

Replication. A gain must show up on evidence the gate never saw: CV folds, the other endpoint, or a held-out split. A sign test across folds is cheap.

9 · Limits and honest reporting¶

  • Uncalibrated: the LLM scores rank patients. They are not risks, and are never reported as such.
  • Cost and drift: two calls per patient, and a hosted model can change under you. Store the raw scores and the model tag with each run.
  • Replicated and failed: scored on the training set, GPT-OSS at weight 0.07 slightly lowers the leak-free CV. Its leaderboard gain was noise on 127 patients.
  • Small gate: 127 public patients hold only a handful of hepatic events, so they resolve only gains larger than most of ours.
  • Leaky CV: the pipeline's CV reads follow-up length. Quote the window-free CV, 0.902, instead.

Generalising the idea¶

Takeaways for Ghanaian researchers¶

  • Start with a model you can run yourself. MedGemma 1.5 (3.3 GB) scored all 1,253 patients on a laptop in about 35 minutes, and no record left the machine.
  • Test cheaply before you pay. Score three patients first, then check rank correlation with your model and the number of distinct scores. Free API tiers allow about 1,000 requests a day, so plan runs around them and save after every call.
  • Judge an LLM by what it adds to your model, on your own held-out data, not by one leaderboard. 127 patients could not tell our gain from noise.
  • Look for leaks in your own features first. Here, the gap between first and last visit ages alone scored 0.96.
  • Know where your data goes. Cloud and free endpoints may log prompts, so check the data terms before sending patient records.

The checklist¶

  • Is the metric a ranking metric? Then blend ranks, not raw scores.
  • Did I drop label proxies and mask data recorded after the event?
  • Does the serialized text carry trajectories and thresholds, not only last values?
  • Is each endpoint asked separately?
  • Is the candidate judged by its gain in the ensemble, not alone?
  • Did I check rank correlation and unique values before paying for every call?
  • Were blend weights fixed on a coarse grid and confirmed on held-out data?
  • Does the gain beat 2 paired SEs on the gate's sample, and the bar for K tries?
  • Is the gain no larger than error independence can deliver?
  • Is every number labelled measured, leaderboard or synthetic?
